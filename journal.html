EssayApr 2025

On the virtues of doing nothing, slowly

In a world obsessed with productivity, the radical act of stillness becomes its own form of resistance. What does it mean to simply be — without agenda, without output?

The essay follows a day built around deliberate idleness: a window left open, a notebook ignored, a walk taken without counting steps. Its argument is quiet but firm: rest is not the opposite of attention. It is often the condition that makes attention possible.

Fontaine asks what kind of self appears when the schedule stops speaking first.

DesignMar 2025

Empty rooms and what they tell us

The Japanese concept of ma — negative space — offers a counter-philosophy to cluttered interiors.

Tanaka moves through tea rooms, galleries, and unfinished apartments to show how space can become a material. The piece treats absence as a design choice rather than a lack.

It is also a small manifesto for restraint: fewer objects, clearer decisions, more room for the body to understand where it is.

PhilosophyJan 2025

The aesthetics of wabi-sabi

Cracks, asymmetry, and the passage of time: how incompleteness became the highest beauty.

This essay traces beauty through repaired bowls, weathered wood, and faces that have stopped pretending time is an enemy. Wabi-sabi becomes less a style than an ethic of tenderness toward change.

Hartmann argues that perfection is often a refusal to participate in life as it actually happens.

Fog as metaphor, fog as fact

On what happens when we lose the horizon — and find something else entirely.

Okonkwo begins with weather and ends with uncertainty. Fog is examined as atmosphere, optical event, and emotional state, a temporary world where certainty loses its authority.

The piece suggests that unclear conditions can still be deeply navigable, if we stop demanding distance before taking the next step.

Walking without destination as practice

Why the unplanned walk might be the most radical thing you can do.

Vasile treats walking as a way of letting the city think through you. There is no route, no step goal, no scenic reward waiting at the end.

What remains is contact: streets, weather, posture, strangers, and the slow correction of a mind that has been moving too fast.

On arriving in places that don't want you

The strange discomfort of being the outsider — and why that's often exactly what we need from travel. Not comfort. Disorientation. The vertigo of the unfamiliar.

This is travel writing without conquest. Fontaine writes against the fantasy that every place should welcome us, translate itself for us, or become a backdrop for our self-discovery.

The better journey, the essay argues, begins when a place remains partly unavailable.

Color is treated here as argument rather than decoration. Tanaka moves from monochrome painting to product design, asking why certain colors feel like moods, doctrines, or entire weather systems.

Hartmann writes about moods as atmospheres rather than problems to solve. The essay resists the impulse to diagnose every feeling and instead asks what a day is asking us to notice.

A field guide to objects that earn their place

Not every beautiful object deserves to stay. Utility, memory, and grace all make different claims.

Tanaka proposes a gentler way to edit the things around us. Instead of asking whether an object sparks joy, the piece asks whether it participates honestly in daily life.

The essay moves through lamps, books, chairs, bowls, tools, and the strange loyalty we develop toward objects that quietly keep showing up for us.

Reading the mountain by its shadows

A trail is not only climbed by the feet. It is read by the eye, the breath, and the changing light.

Okonkwo follows a mountain path from dawn into late afternoon, noticing how shadow redraws the same terrain into several different places.

The piece is about landscape, but also about interpretation: how much of what we see is the world, and how much is the hour?

Marginalia as a second autobiography

The notes we leave in books often reveal the person we were trying to become.

Moreau revisits old annotations and finds a record of former urgencies: underlined sentences, exclamation marks, arguments in pencil, and pages folded for reasons now forgotten.

Reading the margins becomes a way of meeting an earlier self without having to agree with them.
